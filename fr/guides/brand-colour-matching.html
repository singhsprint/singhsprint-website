Will my brand colour actually match? Pantone, thread and ink on custom apparel

Your brand book says one colour. Ink, thread and fabric each have their own opinion about it. Here’s what every decoration method can really do with a Pantone — and the colour spec to send before you order 200 shirts.

9 min read Singhs Print · Updated September 2026 Filed under: Artwork, Ordering

The most expensive phone call in this shop is the one that starts with “the blue is wrong.” Nine times out of ten the blue isn’t wrong — it’s exactly what the artwork specified. What nobody agreed on beforehand was which blue that was: the one on the client’s monitor, the one in their PDF brand book, the one their sign company sprays on vinyl, or the one a thread mill happens to make.

This guide is the conversation we’d rather have before production than after it: the colour systems that matter for apparel, what each decoration method can genuinely hit, and the few lines to put on your order so the first sample is the right one.

Three colour systems, and the one your printer actually needs

Most brand books hand us a colour in a format that was never designed for fabric. Knowing the difference saves a round of samples.

Pantone PMS — the Formula Guide (C and U)

The Pantone Graphics System is the fan deck everyone knows: the current Formula Guide spans roughly 2,600 spot colours across coated and uncoated stock. This is a printing ink standard, built for paper. It’s still the best common language we have with most clients, so it’s what we ask for — but pay attention to the suffix. PANTONE 286 C is that ink on glossy coated paper; PANTONE 286 U is the same ink on uncoated stock, and it reads noticeably flatter and lighter. A cotton t-shirt is matte, absorbent and fibrous. It behaves far more like uncoated paper than coated. If you only ever look at the C chip and then hold a shirt next to it, the shirt will always look “off.” It isn’t. The reference was.

Pantone TCX — the textile standard

TCX chips are dyed on cotton poplin rather than printed on paper, which is why mills and apparel brands specify in TCX: the chip shows how a dye sits in a fibre, not how an ink sits on a page. TPG, its paper-printed sibling, simulates those same colours on coated stock and is cheaper to own, but it can’t reproduce how dye bonds to cotton. If your brand has a TCX reference, send it — it’s the most honest target for apparel. If you don’t own TCX books, the practical fallback is to judge against the uncoated PMS chip.

Hex and RGB

Hex codes come from your website and describe light coming out of a screen, not pigment on cloth — backlit screens can show colours no ink can make. We work from hex all the time, but treat it as a direction rather than a target, and never approve a bulk run off a screenshot on an uncalibrated laptop.

The one-line version: give us a PMS number if you have one, tell us whether it’s C or U, and add the hex as a backup. That single line prevents most colour disputes before they start.

What each method can really do with a Pantone

“Can you match Pantone 186?” has four different answers depending on how the garment is decorated. This is the table we wish more buyers saw before choosing a method.

Why embroidery can only ever get close

This is the single most misunderstood point in custom apparel, so it’s worth being blunt about it. Thread is dyed and spooled before anyone knows what your logo looks like. A standard polyester embroidery range — Isacord 40, for example — carries in the region of 420 solid colours, and Madeira’s Polyneon range is in the same ballpark at 400-plus. Set that against roughly 2,600 Pantone spot colours and the arithmetic is unavoidable: there are simply more Pantones than there are threads. Madeira publishes a Pantone cross-reference and it’s genuinely useful, but the company is upfront that an authorised match doesn’t exist for every Pantone.

So when we say “we’ll match your corporate teal in embroidery,” what we mean is we’ll pull the closest thread in the range and send you the spool number. If your brand colour sits between two threads, you pick which side of it you’d rather live on. That decision belongs to you, not to us, and it takes thirty seconds if we ask early — or a full reorder if we don’t.

Why DTG and DTF have a hard ceiling

Both build colour from process inks. Anything that depends on a pigment outside that set — fluorescent pink, safety-vest yellow-green, gold, silver — cannot be mixed from CMYK, no matter how good the printer is. If your identity leans on a neon or a metallic, plan for screen print with a specialty ink, or accept a deliberate substitution and see it on a sample first.

The garment does half the work

Even with the method settled, the blank underneath changes the result. Four things move colour more than most buyers expect.

Garment colour shows through

Printing anything on a dark garment requires a white underbase — a layer laid down first so the colour has something to sit on. A good underbase gets you most of the way to the chip. A thin one lets the shirt tint the ink, which is why the same red reads brick on black and bright on white. Heather garments are worse: the fabric is a visible mix of two or more fibres, so the ink is never sitting on an even field.

Polyester can push its own dye into your ink

Polyester is coloured with disperse dyes, and heat can move them. Press a light print onto a strongly dyed poly — red and maroon athletic blanks are the classic offenders — and over the following days the garment’s dye can migrate up into the print and tint it pink. It is a real phenomenon, it is manageable with blocker inks and the right temperatures, and it is a reason to tell us the blank before we quote the colour. Our fabric guide goes deeper on which blanks behave.

Texture and lighting

A heavyweight fleece has a longer surface pile than a smooth ring-spun tee, so the same ink reads slightly duller on the hoodie — the eye is seeing shadow between the fibres. Nothing has gone wrong; a hoodie and a tee printed from one file on one day simply won’t read identically side by side. Lighting does the same trick: two colours that agree under our shop lights can diverge under office fluorescents or outdoors. If the apparel has to sit beside an existing branded item — a banner, a vehicle wrap, last year’s jerseys — judge the sample next to that item, in the light where people will actually see them together.

The colour spec to send with your order

Copy this into the notes field of your order or into the email. It takes a minute and it removes almost every source of colour disagreement.

  • The PMS number for each colour in the logo, with the C or U suffix, and the hex as a secondary reference.
  • Vector artwork (.ai, .eps or a vector PDF) with the spot colours named, not flattened into a JPG. Colour lives in the file; a raster export throws the reference away.
  • The exact blank — brand, style number and garment colour. “Navy hoodie” is not a colour spec. Browse the catalog and give us the style number.
  • Which colour is non-negotiable. Most logos have one colour that carries the brand and two that nobody would notice shifting. Tell us which is which.
  • Anything it has to sit beside — existing uniforms, signage, a previous order from another shop.
  • Whether you want a press check. On runs over roughly 100 units we’ll photograph the first pull or the first sewout against your reference and hold production until you reply.

When to insist, and when to let it go

Not every colour deserves the same fight. A rough hierarchy from fifteen-odd years of these conversations:

Insist when the apparel is client-facing and sits alongside other branded assets — dealership polos next to signage, a franchise uniform, a sports club whose jerseys must match a sponsor’s identity. In those cases, spec screen print or DTF, send the PMS, and ask for the press check.

Let it go on event tees, internal swag, one-off charity runs and anything worn once. A thread that’s a half-step off your teal will be noticed by exactly one person in your organisation, and they already know your brand book by heart. Paying a rush premium or delaying an event for a shade nobody else can see is a bad trade.

Decide early on uniform programs you’ll reorder. Whatever thread number or ink formula we land on in round one becomes your standard — we keep it on file so batch four matches batch one. The worst version of this is approving “close enough” on a first order and then discovering two years of staff photos with three different greens in them. If you’re building a program rather than buying a batch, it’s worth one extra day up front. Our decoration durability guide covers the other half of that decision.

What we do by default: send a PMS number and we’ll pull the closest thread or mix the closest ink, then put the number we used in writing on the quote. Send only a hex or a JPG and we’ll ask once — answering that question is the cheapest thing you can do for the order.
